Behavior of substances when dissolved in water
    ELECTROLYTE: substance that, when dissolved in water, produces a solution that conduces electricity
        STRONG ELECTROLYTE: substance that, when dissolved in water, completely dissociates into its ions => solution conduces electricity well
        WEAK ELECTROLYTE: substance that, when dissolved in water, dissociates into its ions only partly => solution conduces electricity less than for a strong electrolyte
    NON-ELECTROLYTE: substance that, when dissolved in water, produces a solution that does not conduce electricity
Chemical equations in aqueous solutions
    Molecular equation: chemical equation written as if substances did not dissociate into ions
        E.g. HCl + NaOH => NaCl + H2O
    Ionic equation: chemical equation that shows individual ions instead of the substances that dissociate
        E.g. H⁺ + Cl⁻ + Na⁺ + OH⁻ => Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + H2O
    Spectator ion: ions that do not participate in the ionic equation
        E.g. Cl⁻ and Na⁺
    Net ionic equation: ionic equation with the spectator ions removed
        E.g. H⁺ + OH⁻ => H2O
NEUTRALIZATION REACTION: reaction between an acid and a base to form compounds that are neither acid nor basic
SALT HYDROLYSIS REACTION: reaction between one of the salt's ions and water. Generally affects pH
Arrhenius theory
    ARRHENIUS ACID: substance that ionizes in water to form protons (H⁺)
    ARRHENIUS BASE: substance that ionizes in water to form hydroxide ions (OH⁻)
    Limitation: spans only aqueous solutions
    Neutralization understood as: acid + base => salt + water
    STRONG ACID: acid that completely ionizes in water (strong electrolyte)
    WEAK ACID: acid that ionizes in water only partly (weak electrolyte)
    STRONG BASE: base that completely ionizes in water (strong electrolyte)
    WEAK BASE: base that ionizes in water only partly (weak electrolyte)
Brønsted-Lowry theory
    Generalizes Arrhenius' theory to not require substances to be in aqueous solution to be acids or bases
    BRONSTED ACID: substance that can donate a proton to another
    BRONSTED BASE: substance that can accept a proton from another
    CONJUGATE BASE: Bronsted acid after it has donated a proton (acid - H⁺)
    CONJUGATE ACID: Bronsted base after it has accepted a proton (base + H⁺)
    ACID-BASE CONJUGATE PAIR: a Bronsted acid or base and its conjugate
    POLYPROTIC ACID: acid that can donate more than one proton
    AMPHOTERIC: substance that may act as an acid or a base. E.g. water
Ion-product of water
    Self-ionization of water: H2O <=> H⁺ + OH⁻
    Occurs naturally for pure water, but with very small concentrations
    Equilibrium constant Kw = 1.0e-14 = [H⁺][OH⁻]
        Holds for all aqueous solutions
    For pure water, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0e-7
    pH = -log10[H⁺]
Dissociation constants of weak acids and weak bases
    Dissociation of a weak acid A: HA <=> H⁺ + A⁻
    Dissociation constant of a weak acid: equilibrium constant Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]
        Ka is a property of each acid
    COMMON-ION EFFECT: the degree of dissociation of a weak acid/base is reduced if we add a substance that shares a common ion
        In the dissociation equation above, if we add a salt that dissociates into A⁻, equilibrium shifted towards reactants
    HENDERSON-HASSELBALCH EQUATION: can be used to approximate the pH of a solution with a weak acid and its corresponding salt
        pKa = -log10(Ka)
        pH = pKa + log10([A⁻]/[HA])
        We can approximate equilibrium concentrations by initial concentrations because the common-ion effect makes dissociation small enough
    Dissociation of a weak base B: B + H2O <=> HB⁺ + OH⁻
    Dissociation constant of a weak base: equilibrium constant Kb = [HB⁺][OH⁻]/[B]
        Same treatment as Ka
    For an acid A and its conjugate base B, Ka * Kb = Kw
        Strong acid => weak conjugate base and vice-versa
    Polyprotic acids have several Ka values, one for each successive ionization
        Ka1 for the 1st proton they lose, Ka2 for the 2nd...
        Kan > Ka(n+1)
    
    
    
TITRATION: procedure to find the concentration of an acid/base by neutralizing it
    TITRAND: in a titration, the compound whose concentration we want to measure
    TITRANT: in a titration, the compound that gets added to neutralize the titrand
