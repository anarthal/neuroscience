Quantum mechanics
    SCHRODINGER EQUATIONS: equations of movement for a quantum system. Akin to Newton's laws, but for quantum mechanics
    WAVE FUNCTION (Ψ): mathematical function describing the state of a quantum system
        Usually accepts (position, time) as arguments, but also possibilities exist (e.g. momentum)
        May return a real or complex number with no direct physical interpretation
        Usually, |Ψ|^2 is the probability density function associated to obtaining the given state for those inputs
            Integrating |Ψ|^2 along all input yields 1
ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION: distribution of electrons for a certain atom/ion
    Atomic nucleus + electrons treated as an isolated quantum system
    ORBITAL: wave function for an electron
        Accepts space and time as parameters, returns a complex value
        |Ψ|^2 is the probability density function of finding the electron at any given time and position
        Plotted as a 3D region => region where the electron has 90% probability of being found
        Orbital may refer to the 3D region, too
    QUANTUM NUMBERS: set of parameters defining orbitals
        Only orbitals with quantum numbers satisfying certain rules are possible
        Each orbital is associated to a certain energy level. Changing from one orbital to another requires exchanging a discrete amount of energy
        PRINCIPAL QUANTUM NUMBER (n): the most influential number. Defines the orbital's size (how far from the nucleus tends the electron to be)
            n = 1, 2, 3...
            For a mono-electronic system, fully defines the orbital's energy
            For a poly-electronic system, l is also required to know the energy level
            ELECTRON SHELL: set of orbitals with the same n
        ANGULAR QUANTUM NUMBER/AZIMUTHAL QUANTUM NUMBER (l): defines the orbital's shape
            l = 0, 1, 2... n-1
            S ORBITAL: orbital with l = 0. Spheric, centered in the nucleus, gets bigger as n increases
            P ORBITAL: orbital with l = 1. Pear-shaped with two lobes rooted in the nucleus
                > energy than the s orbital with the same n
            D ORBITAL: orbital with l = 2
            F ORBITAL: orbital with l = 3
            G ORBITAL: orbital with l = 4
            H ORBITAL: orbital with l = 5
            ELECTRON SUBSHELL: set of orbitals with the same n and l
        MAGNETIC QUANTUM NUMBER (m_l): defines the orbital's spatial orientation
            m_l = -l, -(l - 1), ..., 1, 0, 1, ..., l - 1, l
            1 s orbital, 3 p orbitals, 5 d orbitals, 7 f orbitals...
        SPIN'S QUANTUM NUMBER (m_s): define's the electron magnetic spin (behavior when a magnetic field is applied)
            Does not influence the orbital's energy level or spatial characteristics
            m_s = 1/2 or -1/2
            Each orbital with the same spatial characteristics may be inhabited with two electrons with != spins
        Notation
            u{orbital_name}^v: the orbital with n = u, l = orbital_name has v electrons in it
            E.g. 1s² = orbital with n = 1, l = 0 has 2 electrons in it
            Orbitals sorted by n, then l, rather by the order in which they fill
        Box diagrams
            Each big box is a subshell
            Each small box is an orbital's 3D region (i.e. fixed n, l, m_l)
            Each arrow is an electron. Arrow up for m_s = 1/2, down for -1/2
    How to build electronic configurations
        AUFBAU PRINCIPLE: orbitals with lower energy levels are filled first
            1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, ...
        PAULI EXCLUSION PRINCIPLE: no two electrons can have the same quantum numbers
            I.e. each orbital with the same (n, l, m_l) can fit two electrons with != spin
        HUND'S RULE: when filling a subshell, the most stable configuration is the one with the biggest number of unpaired electrons
            PAIRED ELECTRONS: pair of electrons within the same orbital (same n, l, m_l), but different spin
        These are rules of thumb with exceptions
            Half-filled and fully-filled nd orbitals are particularly stable
            Cu is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹, instead of  [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s²
            Cr is [Ar] 3d10 4s¹, instead of [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s²
    ISOELECTRONIC SPECIES: pair of atoms/ions with the same electronic configuration
Magnetism
    DIAMAGNETIC ATOM: atom or ion that is weakly repelled by a magnetic field
        All electrons are paired in its electronic configuration
    PARAMAGNETIC ATOM: atom or ion that is strongly attracted by a magnetic field
        Some electrons are unpaired in its electronic configuration
PERIODIC PROPERTIES: atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity
    All tendencies shown here are general tendencies with exceptions, specially in transition metals
ATOMIC RADIUS: distance from the nucleus to outermost electrons
    Can't be measured directly. Usually half the distance between atoms in a molecule/ionic crystal
    Increases when going down a group: more filled energy levels
    Decreases when going forward a period: same filled energy levels, but more protons/electrons => electrons drawn closer to nucleus
    IONIC RADIUS: same as atomic radius, but for an ion
        Cations are smaller than the atoms they come from: same number of protons but less electrons
        Anions are bigger than the atoms they come from: same number of protons but more electrons
IONIZATION ENERGY: amount of energy required to extract an electron from an atom, in gas phase
    Measures the tendency of an element to lose electrons
    FIRST IONIZATION ENERGY: ionization energy to extract the first electron
    N-TH IONIZATION ENERGY: ionization energy to extract the N-th atom from a N-1 cation
    Decreases when going down a group: more electrons at higher energy levels, easier to extract
    Increases when going forward a period: TBC
        Exceptions: if removing one electron would yield a specially stable config, ionization energy would decrease
        E.g. Be has 2s^2, B has 2s^2 2p^1. Removing one electron from B yields 2s^2 => Ionization energy for B < Be
        Same for N and O: N < O because removing one electron from O yields a half-full p orbital, stable
    Second ionization energy always > first, but jump is not equal for all elements
        If the initial cation is specially stable (e.g. isoelectronic to a noble gas), then it is big
        E.g. 2nd ionization energy for Li very high
        E.g. 2nd ionization energy for Be small, but 3rd one very high
    Elements with smaller ionization energies tend to appear as cations in nature (e.g. Na in NaCl)
ELECTRON AFFINITY: amount of energy released when an electron is added to a neutral atom, in gas phase
    Measures the tendency of an element to gain electrons
    Varies in the same way as ionization energy for the same reasons
    TBC: exceptions
    Elements with bigger electron affinities tend to appear as anions in nature (e.g. Cl in NaCl)
ELECTRONEGATIVITY: ability of an element to attract electrons in the context of a covalent bond
    When a bond is formed, electrons tend to spend more time closer to the most electronegative atom
    Molecules formed by elements with very different electronegativities form polar compounds
