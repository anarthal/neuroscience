Background
    AMYLASE: enzyme that digests starch
    HAPLOTYPE BLOCK: block within a chromosome where almost no historical recombination has happened
        All SNPs within the block are inherited together
    WGS = Whole Genome Sequencing
Genomics answers 'what can happen?': genetic risk, inherited traits, evolutionary history
GENETIC DISEASE: a disease caused by a change in a DNA sequence
    Germline: mutation inherited from parents
    Acquired: mutation during lifetime
REFERENCE GENOME: fully sequenced genome of a set of individuals used as a coordinate system to define genetic variability
    Human: hg18 (older) and hg38. But does not represent all genetic backgrounds equally
Genetic variation
    SINGLE-NUCLEOTIDE VARIATION (SNV): a deviance from the reference genome in one nucleotide
        SINGLE-NUCLEOTIDE POLYMORPHISM (SNP): a single-nucleotide variant present in at least 1% of the population (i.e. relatively common)
        A SNV does not imply disease
        Epilepsy associated with a SNV in ACh ion channels
        Autism associated with a SNV in synaptic proteins
        B ALLELE: for a biallelic SNV, the least common variant (vs. the one in the reference genome)
    Indels: small insertions/deletions. E.g. intellectual disability associated with indels in neuronal developmental genes
    Tandem repeat expansion: increase in the number of copies of a repetitive sequence of DNA
        Usually causes disease when sequences get too long (over a threshold)
        E.g. Huntington disease
    COPY NUMBER VARIATION (CNV): gain or loss of large DNA segments
        Autism associated with chromosomal micro-deletions
        Schizophrenia associated with duplications in genes affecting synaptic balance
        Genetic backgrounds where potatoes were part of the diet have more amylase gene copies
    Structural variants: duplication, deletion or transferral of a chromosome or large parts of it
        E.g. neural development disorders
    Regulatory mutations: mutations in regulatory sequences => alter quantity or timing of expression
    Only ~0.5% of the genome is variable
Study procedures
    COMPARATIVE GENOMIC HYBRIDIZATION (CGH): technique to study CNVs and structural variants qualitatively across the chromosomes
        Patient's DNA and reference DNA, fluorescently labelled with != colors (e.g. red and green), 1:1 proportion
        Metaphase chromosomes (normal DNA), no labelling
        Labelled and unlabelled samples competitively hybridize with normal DNA and produce a signal across each chromosome
        Study relative signal intensity for each color => detect variations
            E.g. yellow: normal, green: deletion, red: gain
        Can't detect balanced variations (e.g. translocations or reversals)
    ARRAY-BASED COMPARATIVE GENOMIC HYBRIDIZATION (aCGH): technique to check for a set of CNVs in a genome
        Like CGH, but metaphase chromosomes are replaced by short DNA fragments where suspected CNVs may happen
        Each array cell targets a specific CNV
        Signal intensity proportional to the amount of DNA
        Usual signal representation: log2 R = log2(sample_intensity/reference_intensity)
    SNP ARRAY: technique to check whether a set of SNPs is present in a genome
        Works for biallelic variants: assumes that the population has either allele A or B
        Probes: fluorescently labelled sequences matching either allele A (one color) or allele B (!= color) for one SNP
        Each array cell contains both probes targeting one SNP
        Patient's DNA is amplified by PCR, fixed to the array and hybridized to the probes
        Resulting signal
            Single color (e.g. red or green): homozygous for that allele
            Color mix (e.g. yellow): heterozygous
            No signal: unknown. May be due to technical error, rare 3rd allele
        Modern SNP arrays quantify the signal for each allele, Ia and Ib => in general better than aCGH
        B ALLELE FREQUENCY (BAF): for a biallelic SNV being sequenced, the proportion of the obtained signal supporting the B allele
            BAF = Ib / (Ia + Ib)
            Used for genotyping
            Homozygous A: ~0.0
            Heterozygous: ~0.5
            Homozygous B: ~1.0
        LOG R RATIO (LRR): ratio between the total signal and the expected signal if there were only two copies of the DNA under study
            LRR = log2 ((Ia + Ib) / I_expected)
            Used to detect CNVs
            2 copies: ~0
            Insertion: >0
            Deletion: <0
        Cheaper and more scalable than sequencing, but targets only known variants
    Sequencing
        VARIANT ALLELE FREQUENCY (VAF): when sequencing a SNV, the proportion of reads supporting a particular letter
            VAF = num_variant_reads / total_reads
            Low VAF does not imply biology, it can be because of sequencing errors
        READ DEPTH/COVERAGE: the amount of times that a particular base has been sequenced (i.e. appears in a read)
            30x: a base appears in 30 reads

GENOME-WIDE ASSOCIATION STUDY (GWAS): observational study that tries to find associations between genome variants and a trait (e.g. disease)
    E.g. which genes may be associated with Alzheimer's disease?
    May use data from the technologies described above (e.g. sequencing or SNP arrays)
    Outputs risk, not certainty
    MANHATTAN PLOT: plot of all SNPs in an study, with X = chromosome position, Y = -log10(P)
        P = statistical significance of the association of the SNP to the trait under study
        Higher points => higher significance
    LINKAGE DISEQUILIBRIUM (LD): effect showing several nearby SNPs as linked to a trait because contiguous traits are segregated together
        Some of these SNPs may be biologically relevant, the others are just artifacts => complicates interpreting results
        LD heatmap: shows correlation between pairs of SNPs
    Conclusions don't tell anything about cell type
POLYGENIC RISK SCORE: relative risk of an individual with a certain genetic background of suffering a disease vs. a control group
