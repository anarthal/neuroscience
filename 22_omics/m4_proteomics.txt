Background
    PROTEOFORMS: each of the proteins derived from a single gene. May be different due to alternative splicing and PTMs
    AMPHOLYTE: molecule with both acid and basic groups. Can act as either depending on pH (e.g. amino acids)
    ISOTOPE: set of atoms belonging to the same chemical elements, but with != mass (i.e. != num neutrons)
Why proteomics
    mRNA levels don't always correlate with protein concentration (mRNA stability, protein degradation)
    From a single mRNA, multiple proteoforms (post-translational modifications)
Difficulties
    No equivalent to PCR for proteomics
    Proteome is more varied and more dynamic than genome/transcriptome
    Wide range of concentrations
Approaches
    Expression proteomics: how much protein is expressed in each cell type?
    Structural proteomics
        Distribution in intracellular compartments
        Which post-translational modifications does the protein have?
        How does it interact with other proteins?
    Functional proteomics: what does this protein do?
Analysis levels
    BOTTOM-UP PROTEOMICS: approach where proteins are digested into peptides < 30 aas, then analyzed
        Not good to profile the entire proteome (too complex), better for targeted proteomics
        Tools are well developed
        Peptide to protein inference difficult
        Better separate before digestion
        SHOTGUN PROTEOMICS: bottom-up approach where a protein mixture is digested and analyzed all together (less targeted than usual approaches)
    MIDDLE-DOWN PROTEOMICS: approach where proteins are digested into peptides > 30 aas, then analyzed
    TOP-DOWN PROTEOMICS: approach where proteins are analyzed intact
        Can get PTMs
        Not supported for all proteins
        Tools less developed
        Sample preparation more critical
TARGETED PROTEOMICS: method to detect and quantify one (or several) known proteins (e.g. drug testing)
    SELECTED REACTION MONITORING (SRM): MS technique for targeted proteomics where a single protein is filtered and analyzed
        Usually ESI-QqQ
        Both the 1st and 2nd detector filter for specific m/z values
    MULTIPLE REACTION MONITORING (MRM): MS technique for targeted proteomics where multiple proteins are filtered and analyzed
        Usually ESI-QqQ
        The 1st detector filters for one m/z value. The 2nd selects multiple
        Ion transition: each of the filters in the 2nd analyzer
        Cheaper than PRM, usually for drug testing
    PARALLEL REACTION MONITORING (PRM): MS technique for targeted proteomics where the entire sample is analyzed at the same time
        Usually orbitrap
Separation: whole-cell lysate is too complex => separating reduces complexity
    Electrophoresis
        1D-SDS-PAGE: see 22_lab_techniques/11_sds_page.txt
            Bad resolution: every band has lots of proteins
            Not enough for whole cell lysates, but may be enough for CSF
        ISOELECTRIC FOCUSING (IEF): separating method according to isoelectric point (pI)
            When subjected to an electric field in a pH gradient, molecules migrate until reaching their pI
        2D-SDS-PAGE: separating method using isoelectric focusing first, then SDS-PAGE
            IMMOBILIZED PH GRADIENT (IPG) STRIP: plastic strip with a pH gradient, for isoelectric focusing. Acts as stacking gel
            Conventional staining
            Staining might interfere with downstream analysis
            Not very reproducible
            Large, hydrophobic proteins don't separate well during isoelectric focusing
            Different proteoforms may have != pI => != bands
            Can't detect low concentration proteins
        PREPARATIVE ISOELECTRIC FOCUSING: isoelectric focusing that can separate a high quantity of proteins (vs. analytical)
            Usually in solution. pH gradient produced by soluble ampholytes
        CAPILLARY ELECTROPHORESIS: isoelectric focusing across a microscopic capillary
            Small sample concentrations
            More precise and reproducible
            Can be coupled to mass spectrometry
    CHROMATOGRAPHY: separation method family based on interaction times between a stationary and a mobile phase
        RETENTION TIME: in chromatography, period of time that each analyte takes to exit the stationary phase
        Mobile phase may be liquid or gas
        Samples interacting more move more slowly
        CHROMATOGRAM: plots the chromatograph's detector signal vs. time (visual output of a chromatography)
        HIGH-PERFORMANCE LIQUID CHROMATOGRAPHY (HPLC): generic term for modern chromatography where the mobile phase is liquid (uses pumps instead of gravity)
        REVERSE PHASE LIQUID CHROMATOGRAPHY: stationary phase is non-polar, mobile phase is polar (e.g. proteins solved in water)
            Separates proteins based on hydrophobicity. More hydrophobic proteins interact more => elute later
        NORMAL PHASE LIQUID CHROMATOGRAPHY: stationary phase is polar, mobile phase is non-polar (e.g. organic solvent)
            Separates proteins based on hydrophobicity. More hydrophilic proteins interact more => elute later
        SIZE EXCLUSION CHROMATOGRAPHY: stationary phase are beads with pores (e.g. agarose)
            Separates proteins based on shape and size. Smaller molecules get into the pores => interact more => elute later
        GAS CHROMATOGRAPHY: mobile phase is a gas, stationary phase is solid or liquid
            Gas should be inert, e.g. He
Digestion: divide the protein into small peptide sequences that can be analyzed by MS
    Short sequences better for MS analysis: more sensitivity, less absolute error
    Some large, hydrophobic proteins can't be analyzed intact
    TRYPSIN: protease that cleaves at lysine (K) and arginine (R) unless followed by a proline (P)
        Used in proteomics because it's well characterized => we can simulate digestions
    Non-specific proteases: better coverage but we can't simulate digestion
MASS SPECTROMETRY: technique to measure the mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio of a molecule
    Ions subjected to an electric/magnetic field are deflected differently depending on their m/z
        More mass => deflected less
        More charge => deflected more
    Vacuum important in instrumentation (avoid interference from gas molecules)
    MOLECULAR ION: the ion originated from the precursor molecule (molecule to analyze), without breaking it
    FRAGMENT ION: ions created by breaking the precursor molecule
    Ion source: generates ions from the molecule to analyze
        HARD IONIZATION: high-energy ionization methods that destroy the molecular ion and generate many fragments => for small molecules
        SOFT IONIZATION: low-energy ionization methods that don't destroy the molecular ion => for big biomolecules
        ELECTRON IMPACT (EI): hard ionization method where molecules are bombarded with electrons => end up losing electrons
        FAST ATOM BOMBARDMENT (FAB): another hard ionization method
        ELECTROSPRAY IONIZATION (ESI): soft ionization method where compounds in solution are applied a high voltage and nebulize in a spray
            Produces ions with multiple charges => avoids too big m/z values => helps instruments
            Positive ion mode: compound gains an H+. For basic compounds
            Negative ion mode: compound loses an H+. For acid compounds
            The most sensitive (works with low concentrations)
        MATRIX-ASSISTED LASER DESORPTION IONIZATION (MALDI): soft ionization method where compounds crystalize in a matrix and are extracted with a laser
            Generates singly charged ions
    Mass analyzer: generates the m/z signal by manipulating ions
        RESOLUTION: smallest increment in the measured quantity that an instrument can detect
            Measured as increment/absolute_magnitude (e.g. ppm)
        ACCURACY: for an instrument, difference between the real and the measured quantity
        SENSITIVITY: smallest analyte concentration that an instrument can work with
        TANDEM MS (MS/MS): coupling two analyzers in series to achieve better m/z resolution
            1. Select ions within a specific m/z range (1st analyzer)
            2. Fragment the selected ions
            3. Select ions again and analyze them (2nd analyzer)
        QUADRUPOLE: mass filter that selects a narrow range of m/z ions
            4 rods are applied an oscillating voltage => selects ions based on their response
            Scanning: ions flow continuously
        COLLISION-INDUCED DISSOCIATION (CID): technique to fragment ions in a gas phase using collisions
        TRIPLE QUADRUPOLE (QqQ): tandem MS setup using a quadrupole for selecting, a collision cell, and another quadrupole for analyzing
            Low resolution but effective mass filter => for quantitative analysis (i.e. how much protein do I have?)
        ION TRAP: chamber that stores, fragments and selects ions in discrete cycles
            Low resolution, but higher than QqQ
            Better for qualitative analysis (i.e. identification, sequencing)
        TIME OF FLIGHT (TOF): accelerates ions and separates them based on the time it takes for them to get to the detector
            High resolution
            Usually with MALDI
            Can be coupled with a quadrupole (Q-TOF) or another TOF (TOF-TOF) for better resolution
        FOURIER TRANSFORM ION CYCLOTRON (FT-ICR): instrument that analyzes all ions simultaneously, without selection
            Makes the ions orbit with a magnetic field and applies a pulse => measures current generated by their response => Fourier => spectrum
            High resolution
        ORBITRAP: instrument that analyzes all ions simultaneously, without selection
            Similar to FT-ICR, but uses an electric field
            High resolution, but worse than FT-ICR
    Detector: captures the m/z signal produced by the analyzer
        FARADAY CUP: detector consisting of a conductive cup that collects ions => current proportional to num impacting ions
        DYNODE: metal electrode that emits electrons when impacted by an ion (electron multiplier) => multiplies the signal
            Refinement of the Faraday cup
        MICROCHANNEL PLATE DETECTOR (MCP): plate with small conduits, each acting as a dynode => spatial resolution
    MATRIX EFFECTS: inaccuracies in measurement when the target analyte is contaminated with other compounds (i.e. is in a matrix, like blood)
        Upstream analysis methods (i.e. detergents) can also produce this
        ION SUPPRESSION: matrix effect where a contaminant decreases the target analyte's signal
    Spectrum interpretation
        BASE PEAK: highest peak in the MS spectrum. Doesn't have to be the precursor peak
        MOLECULAR ION PEAK/PRECURSOR PEAK: peak in the MS spectrum corresponding to the molecular ion
        Any peak at m/z = M corresponding to an ion containing C12 has an adjacent peak corresponding to C13
            1% of all C atoms is C13 => the C13 peak is smaller
            M and M+1 peaks separated by 1/z (mass difference of 1)
            M+1 peak intensity proportional to the number of C atoms
        ADDUCT ION: ion formed by merging the molecular ion with another atom or compound (e.g. [M+Na]+)
            Can create peaks with m/z > the molecular ion's m/z

Protein identification
    Adducts: additional peaks that appear when other compounds (e.g. water, acetic acid) gets integrated in the ions
    Peptide mass fingerprinting: we get the mass spec of peptide fragments => database
        Cleave usually with trypsin => Cut at lysine or arginine, if they're not next to an arginine
        Virtual digestion => From a protein with a known sequence, get the peptides that we may get
        From the sequence mass => Database => Set of protein hits
        The more tolerance we have in mass measurements, the more hits we have
        The more peptides we analyze, the narrower the search
        Good for pure proteins (e.g. confirm 'is this the protein I want?'). But messy for protein mixtures
        Ambiguity: e.g. amino acid rearrange => == mass
        PTMs will fail to match => you may need to remove modifications (e.g. phosphorylations via a phosphatase)
        For identification, not for sequencing
    Sequencing with tandem MS
        Every amino-acid has a != mass
        Peptides usually break through the peptide bond
        If we only get one charge to the molecule
            b-ion: the N-terminus fragment of the molecule
            y-ion: the C-terminus fragment
        A given sequence that breaks generates a given mass spectrum
            Assign peaks y_(N-1), y_(N-2) to the ones in the right
            The difference in mass tells the amino-acid
            Same for the b indices
            With the two sequences, you get the full motif
            Different sequences break in different ways depending on sequence
    identification with tandem MS
PPI
    Bait experiment: immobilize one protein, then elute and separate => all proteins that might interact with the immobilized protein
        Reverse bait experiment: immobilize the other proteins obtained in the bait experiment, to confirm interaction
        You might do this along all the proteins in a complex to obtain a network
        But requires immobilizing the protein => time consuming, and might cause loss of protein function
    Alternative: immunoprecipìtation
    Predictions
        Motif-based: find motifs that are known to interact with another
        Sequence-based: like motifs, but more general
        Codon-usage: the ones with more favorable codon usage will probably be expressed more
        Close in the genome
        Co-presence: proteins that interact are either all present or all absent in a genome. Compare with other genomes to find orthologs (same gene, != species)
        Fusion: if two genes in one species were fused in another, these proteins likely interact
        Keeping the order: if two proteins interact, their genes will likely be in the same order in all species
        Functional similarity: if they share pathways in gene ontology, they probably interact
        Incorrect assignment of orthologs leads to failure
        Proteins in the same functional network probably interact
        Difficult to apply in eukaryotes
        An interaction may happen without any of these signals
        Can't tell if it's a functional relation or a physical interaction
Mapping modifications
    Requires more coverage than identification, as modifications may happen in any Aa
    Tools need to be specified which modifications we expect => can deduce modifications and where by the mass difference between modified and unmodified ions
    E.g. phosphorylation adds 80 units
    All the y/b ions containing the modification will be displaced from the predicted value by the weight of the modification
    Difficult because the non-modified form is usually more abundant
    Enrichment: technique to make the modified version more abundant
        E.g. use antibodies that target the phosphorylated site and analyze this
        




Nuclear magnetic resonance: alternative to MS. Less sensitive, but more reproducible, and less destructive
